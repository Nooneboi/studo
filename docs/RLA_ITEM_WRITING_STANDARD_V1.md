# Studo RLA Item-Writing Standard — v1.0

> Internal authoring standard for GED-authentic RLA practice. This document defines how Studo creates original assessment items that align to the published GED RLA construct without copying secure or copyrighted operational test content.

## 1. Non-negotiable assessment model

- **Source-based:** exam-style RLA items must be tied to a passage or multiple source texts. Short stand-alone drills are allowed only when clearly labeled as *training*, not as exam simulation.
- **Passage-set model:** exam-style reading sets should normally use a 400–900 word stimulus and roughly 6–8 associated items.
- **Passage mix:** maintain an overall bank close to **75% informational / 25% literary**. Informational material should rotate across science, social studies/civics, workplace, and community contexts.
- **Cognitive demand:** use DOK 1–3 according to the official target range. Do not make items “hard” through confusing wording.
- **Multiple choice:** exam-style MC uses four options and remains passage-based.
- **Editing:** authentic editing sets should use contextual workplace/community passages, generally 350–450 words, with embedded choice/drop-down behavior where the UI supports it.
- **Extended Response:** full ER uses two source texts, no more than about 650 words total, and is designed as a 45-minute analytical writing task.
- **ER scoring:** feedback must separately address argument/evidence, development/organization, and clarity/standard English conventions.

## 2. What “GED-authentic” means for Studo

We match the **construct, cognitive demand, passage environment, item logic, option logic, and response mode**. We do **not** imitate by paraphrasing a released question line-by-line or by reproducing secure items. Every Studo item must be newly authored from original, public-domain, or appropriately licensed material.

## 3. Passage requirements

### 3.1 Reading passages
- 400–900 words for exam-style passage sets.
- High-school/career-and-college-ready complexity; readability alone is not enough—syntax, idea density, structure, and vocabulary-in-context matter.
- Informational passages should feel like real documents/articles, not synthetic “test paragraphs.”
- Literary excerpts should feel complete enough to support character, event, theme, inference, and language questions.

### 3.2 Informational context rotation

Use a planned rotation rather than repeatedly writing generic school topics:

1. **Science:** human health/living systems; energy/conservation; accessible science reporting.
2. **Social studies/civics:** civic ideas, public policy, historical correspondence/speeches, the “Great American Conversation.”
3. **Workplace/community:** memos, customer letters, policy notices, procedural documents, event notices, letters to the editor, public postings.

### 3.3 Passage annotation before item writing

Before writing any question, annotate:
- explicit key facts
- central/main idea(s)
- supporting evidence anchors
- inference opportunities
- relationships / cause / sequence
- word/phrase meaning opportunities
- tone and point-of-view cues
- paragraph/section functions
- claims, reasons, evidence, assumptions, counterarguments (if argumentative)
- plausible learner misconceptions

If the passage does not naturally support a target, **do not force a question onto it**.

## 4. Item-writing workflow

1. Choose the exact skill ID and official target.
2. Choose passage type/context.
3. Set intended DOK **before** writing the stem.
4. Define the evidence anchor or reasoning path for the correct answer.
5. Draft the stem in plain, direct language.
6. Write the correct answer independently.
7. Build distractors from real misconceptions—not random wrong statements.
8. Verify there is one clearly best answer.
9. Write the short learner explanation.
10. Create transfer variants using a different text/context.
11. Editorial review and ambiguity check.

## 5. Stem standard

- Keep the stem shorter than the reasoning required to solve it.
- Ask one task at a time.
- Prefer direct forms such as “Which statement best…?”, “How does…?”, “Why does the author…?”, “What can the reader reasonably infer…?”
- Avoid trivia phrasing, double negatives, trick wording, and unnecessary vocabulary.
- Avoid asking for a terminology label when the real construct is interpretation or reasoning. Example: test the *effect* of a rhetorical move more often than the name of the device.

## 6. Distractor standard

Every wrong answer must have a **reason a real learner might choose it**. Recommended distractor families include:

- `mentioned_not_supported` — appears in the text but does not answer the question
- `too_broad` — exceeds what the passage supports
- `too_narrow` — captures only one detail
- `partly_true` — contains some truth but fails the full task
- `evidence_for_other_claim` — legitimate evidence, wrong target
- `unsupported_inference` — plausible world knowledge, insufficient text support
- `reversed_relationship` — cause/effect, viewpoint, or sequence reversed
- `summary_not_analysis` — restates content when analysis is required
- `personal_opinion` — sounds reasonable but is not text-grounded
- language-specific traps such as nearest-noun agreement, homophones, comma splices, and ambiguous pronouns.

**Ban:** joke answers, absurd answers, options that differ only by obvious grammar when grammar is not the construct, and distractors that can be eliminated without reading the passage.

## 7. Difficulty model

Use six dimensions already represented in Studo metadata:

| Dimension | Low | Medium | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| Text complexity | simple syntax/ideas | mixed syntax + moderate density | denser syntax/abstract relations |
| Reasoning depth | locate / recognize | connect 2+ clues | synthesize/evaluate multiple relations |
| Evidence distance | same sentence | nearby paragraph | dispersed across text/sources |
| Distractor similarity | clearly weaker | plausible | strongly plausible but textually distinguishable |
| Source count | one | one with graphic | multiple sources |
| Response demand | select | classify/revise | extended analysis/write |

**Hard ≠ confusing.** Difficulty must come from the construct.

## 8. Passage-set blueprint

An authentic single-passage set should mix targets instead of asking six versions of the same question. Example:

```text
Q1 Explicit meaning          DOK 1
Q2 Main idea                 DOK 2
Q3 Inference                 DOK 2
Q4 Supporting evidence       DOK 2
Q5 Meaning in context        DOK 2
Q6 Paragraph function        DOK 2–3
Q7 Author purpose / POV      DOK 2
```

Argument passages may replace some of these with claim/evidence/reasoning targets. Multi-source sets should deliberately require cross-source integration.

## 9. Training vs Practice vs Quiz vs Exam

| Layer | Purpose | Allowed simplification |
|---|---|---|
| Study resource | Teach the skill | direct worked examples, notes, annotations |
| Practice | Apply one skill with support | shorter texts, hints, immediate explanations |
| Quiz | Check recent learning | limited help, fresh variants |
| Exam-style set | Simulate GED conditions | source-based, authentic passage length/format, no teaching during attempt |
| Train Me | Retrieval and transfer | fresh family variants chosen by learner history |

## 10. Explanation standard

First screen after an answer:

1. **Correct / Not quite**
2. Correct answer if needed
3. **Why** — 1–3 plain sentences linking the answer to the text
4. Optional **Answer breakdown** — why the selected distractor fails, evidence location, and one reusable rule

Explanations must explain the reasoning, not merely repeat the correct option.

## 11. Question-family rule

A family is a repeatable reasoning pattern. Each serious skill needs multiple families and each family needs transfer variants. The learner should not be able to “master” a skill by remembering one passage.

**Initial production target:**
- P0 skill: 4–6 families
- P1 skill: 3–5 families
- P2/extension skill: 2–3 families
- Each family: minimum 3 genuinely different transfer variants before strong mastery claims

## 12. QA gate

Before publication, reviewer must answer **yes** to all of the following:

- Does the item measure the intended skill—not another easier skill?
- Is the passage sufficient to answer without outside knowledge?
- Is there exactly one best answer?
- Can the correct answer be defended with a precise evidence path?
- Is every distractor plausible for a known misconception?
- Are options parallel in grammar, length, and level of specificity where practical?
- Is difficulty caused by reasoning rather than confusing prose?
- Is the explanation short, accurate, and reusable?
- Does the family have a plan for fresh transfer variants?
- Are source/rights records complete?

## 13. Field-test rule

When learner data becomes available, review items using response distributions. Flag for revision if:

- a distractor is almost never selected
- two options split strong learners unusually evenly
- a “hard” item is nearly universally correct
- a supposedly easy item produces widespread failure
- learner explanations/comments reveal an ambiguity

Performance data informs revision; it does not override content review.

### R1.1 — Explicit meaning
**Domain:** Core Meaning  
**Official targets:** R.2.1  
**DOK:** 1–2  
**Preferred item types:** multiple_choice

- **`explicit.fact.locate`** — Locate an explicitly stated fact or idea
  - Stem pattern: *Which statement is directly supported by the passage?*
  - Typical distractor logic: inference_not_stated, mentioned_wrong_detail, contradicts_text
- **`explicit.detail.match`** — Match a question to the exact detail that answers it
  - Stem pattern: *According to the passage, which statement explains ___?*
  - Typical distractor logic: related_not_answering, true_elsewhere, unsupported_extension

### R1.2 — Main idea / central idea
**Domain:** Core Meaning  
**Official targets:** R.2.1, R.2.4  
**DOK:** 1–3  
**Preferred item types:** multiple_choice

- **`mainidea.whole.explicit`** — Identify the central idea when the text states it fairly directly
  - Stem pattern: *Which statement best expresses the central idea of the passage?*
  - Typical distractor logic: supporting_detail, too_broad, too_narrow
- **`mainidea.whole.implied`** — Infer the central idea from several details
  - Stem pattern: *Which statement best expresses an idea developed throughout the passage?*
  - Typical distractor logic: single_detail, reasonable_but_incomplete, unsupported_generalization

### R1.3 — Supporting details
